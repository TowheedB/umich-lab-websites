Research

What we study

Understanding how cells sort proteins and maintain quality control within the secretory pathway.

Overview

The Chang Lab investigates the cellular mechanisms that maintain protein quality control at different organellar sites, using genetic and biochemical approaches in the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae. A long-standing question is how proteins are sorted in the secretory pathway: the lab studies conformationally impaired variants of the plasma membrane ATPase Pma1 that are handled differently by quality control systems, being degraded in the ER, routed to endosomes, or internalized from the cell surface. Forward genetic suppressor screens are used to identify the machinery that recognizes misfolded proteins in each pathway. More recent work focuses on how ER stress is communicated to mitochondria and peroxisomes, showing that increased mitochondrial respiration and retrograde signaling promote survival from ER stress, and on how Orm proteins integrate signals to maintain sphingolipid homeostasis.

Research themes

4 active directions

  1. Pma1 localization and quality control

    Studies how the yeast plasma membrane ATPase Pma1 is sorted and processed, examining mutant variants that are degraded in the ER, routed to endosomes for destruction, or improperly internalized from the cell surface.

  2. Genetic suppression analysis

    Uses forward genetics to isolate suppressor mutations that rescue defective pma1 variants, revealing the molecular machinery that recognizes misfolded proteins in different quality control pathways.

  3. ER stress, mitochondria, and peroxisomes

    Examines how misfolding in the ER drives mitochondrial restructuring and retrograde signaling, and how peroxisomal support of mitochondrial respiration promotes survival from ER stress.

  4. Sphingolipid homeostasis

    Investigates how the conserved ER membrane proteins Orm1 and Orm2 integrate multiple signals to regulate sphingolipid synthesis, and how sphingolipid accumulation leads to mitochondrial dysregulation and cell death.
