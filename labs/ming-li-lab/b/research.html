Research

What we study

Understanding how lysosomes maintain cellular quality control and how these systems fail in disease.

Overview

The Ming Li Lab investigates the lysosome: how it is born (biogenesis), how it is regulated to stay functional (quality control), and how it communicates with other organelles. The lab discovered that damaged or misregulated lysosomal membrane proteins are ubiquitinated and internalized by the ESCRT machinery into the lumen for degradation, and it has identified E3 ligase complexes responsible for substrate recognition in yeast and human cells. Through CRISPR screening the lab identified TMEM251 (LYSET) as a Golgi protein essential for the mannose-6-phosphate pathway, whose mutation causes a severe skeletal dysplasia (mucolipidosis type V). Other projects examine ER-associated degradation of mutant cystinosin in cystinosis, where chemical chaperones partially rescue folding and reduce cystine accumulation by about 70 percent, and how cancer cells upregulate lysosome numbers to support rapid growth. The lab uses budding yeast, human cancer cell lines, and vertebrate models including zebrafish and mice.

Research themes

4 active directions

  1. Lysosome membrane quality control via ubiquitin and ESCRT-dependent microautophagy

    Studies how damaged or mutated lysosomal membrane proteins are recognized by E3 ligases, ubiquitinated, and internalized by ESCRT machinery for degradation, from yeast vacuoles to human lysosomes, including reconstitution of lysosomal transporters such as Ypq1.

  2. TMEM251 and mucolipidosis type V

    Characterizes TMEM251, a small Golgi membrane protein identified by CRISPR screening that regulates the M6P pathway for lysosomal enzyme trafficking; current work examines TMEM251-GNPT interactions and zebrafish knockout models.

  3. ERAD in cystinosis pathogenesis

    Examines how cystinosin mutations cause misfolding and ER-associated degradation, leading to cystine accumulation and kidney and eye damage; chemical chaperones partially rescue the mutant, and the lab is developing mouse models and screening other unstable variants.

  4. Lysosome biogenesis in cancer

    Explores how cancer cells dramatically increase lysosome numbers to meet nutrient demands, using genome-wide CRISPR screening and live-cell imaging.
