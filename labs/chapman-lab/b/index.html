Matthew Chapman, PI

chapmanm@umich.edu

Investigating how bacteria build functional amyloid fibers and organize microbial communities.

The Chapman Lab studies how bacteria integrate and adapt to their environment by investigating curli, extracellular amyloid fibers produced by Escherichia coli and certain Salmonella species. Curli are structurally and biochemically similar to the amyloid fibers associated with human diseases such as Alzheimer's and Huntington's, making bacterial curli a tractable model for understanding amyloid formation. The lab uses microscopy, biochemistry, and genetics to study how aggregation-prone curli subunits are safely transported to the cell surface and polymerized into stable fibers, and how chaperones such as CsgC prevent toxic aggregation. Additional work examines the bacterial extracellular matrix and its contribution to biofilm resistance, rationally designed anti-amyloid and anti-biofilm compounds, and the evolutionary economics of producing extracellular proteins.

Research

  1. Curli biogenesis and amyloid assembly

    Studies how aggregation-prone curli subunits are transported from the cell interior to the surface, where they polymerize into stable amyloid fibers, using microscopy, biochemistry, and genetics.

  2. Protein and chemical chaperones

    Characterizes molecules that limit amyloid aggregation, including the bacterial chaperone CsgC, and investigates rationally designed 2-pyridone compounds as anti-amyloid and anti-biofilm agents.

  3. Bacterial extracellular matrix biology

    Examines the molecular pathways controlling extracellular matrix components and their role in biofilm resistance to antibiotics and immune responses.

  4. Protein economics and evolution

    Explores how evolutionary pressures shape protein design, including the synthetic costs of producing extracellular proteins in microorganisms.
