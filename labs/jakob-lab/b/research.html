Research

What we study

Investigating how cells sense and survive oxidative stress across health, defense, and aging.

Overview

The Jakob lab works in two broad areas: understanding the events early in life that set the mechanisms governing aging and long-term stress resistance, and uncovering the functions of the ancient and ubiquitous biopolymer inorganic polyphosphate. The lab studies how reactive oxygen species accumulate during aging, host defense, and disease, and how thiol-based redox switches in proteins such as the chaperone Hsp33 allow cells to respond. Work on developmental ROS has shown that transient oxidative events early in life individualize organismal stress resistance and lifespan through changes in the histone landscape. Polyphosphate studies span its roles in amyloidogenesis, bacterial heterochromatin and phase-separated condensates, iron chelation, and tumorigenesis. The lab uses biochemical, structural, and cell biological approaches in E. coli, Vibrio cholerae, S. cerevisiae, C. elegans, and mammalian cells, including mass spectrometry-based thiol trapping.

Research themes

4 active directions

  1. Redox-regulated chaperones

    Studies thiol-based redox switches in proteins such as the molecular chaperone Hsp33, which is activated by oxidative stress to protect cells, and how bleach and other oxidants act as physiological antimicrobials.

  2. ROS and lifespan

    Investigates how reactive oxygen species generated early in development alter the histone landscape and set long-term stress resistance and lifespan in C. elegans.

  3. ROS in cell fate decisions

    Examines how redox heterogeneity influences cell fate decisions, including in mouse embryonic stem cells.

  4. Polyphosphate in amyloidogenesis and cell biology

    Explores how inorganic polyphosphate modulates amyloid formation, bacterial heterochromatin and RNA-protein condensates, iron chemistry, and tumorigenesis.
