Research

What we study

Discovering how cells detect, repair, and survive damage to their genomes.

Overview

The Simmons Lab investigates how bacteria duplicate and repair their DNA, using Bacillus subtilis as a model organism, with an emphasis on mechanisms that are conserved in eukaryotes and humans. Current projects include the biochemistry of RNase H enzymes that remove ribonucleotides and RNA-DNA hybrids from the genome, and the use of mutation accumulation lines with whole-genome resequencing to identify mutagenic signatures linked to antibiotic resistance. The lab also uses transposon-insertion sequencing screens to discover uncharacterized genes required for surviving DNA damage, and SMRT sequencing to map N6-methyladenosine sites that regulate gene expression and virulence in B. subtilis and Streptococcus pyogenes. Approaches span genetics, biochemistry, structural biology, genomics, and single-molecule imaging in collaboration with the Biteen lab.

Research themes

3 active directions

  1. Ribonucleotide excision repair and RNA-DNA hybrids

    Biochemical characterization of RNase H enzymes that remove RNA from RNA-DNA hybrids in bacterial cells, combined with mutation accumulation lines and whole-genome resequencing to identify mutagenic signatures of uncorrected ribonucleotides and their connection to antibiotic resistance.

  2. Discovery of gene function in DNA damage survival

    Transposon-insertion mutagenesis followed by deep sequencing identifies uncharacterized genes that contribute to survival of DNA damage, addressing the fact that 46.2% of B. subtilis genes have no demonstrated experimental function.

  3. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) regulation of gene expression

    Pacific Biosciences SMRT sequencing maps m6A methylation sites in B. subtilis and Streptococcus pyogenes genomes, revealing how methylation regulates virulence gene expression and bacterial adherence to human cells through changes in RNA polymerase occupancy.
